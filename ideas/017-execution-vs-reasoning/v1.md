New article for reasoning versus execution. 
Can you pat your head and rub your stomach? It’s pretty hard. 

Agents have the same challenge 'reasoning' and execution (writing code) at the same time.

Reasoning is when its deeply thinking about a particular thing.

Execution is when its doing a particular thing - tool calls .

Agents are very good at executing a set of instructions. They are also very good at thinking through a problem.

The problem is that many of us, when asking it to do something, are asking it to do both things at once.

When you ask an agent to do a task, but you don't 'trigger' it to go into thinking mode first, then it will start and stay in execution mode.

This is one of the problems developers run into - "The agent didn't use the function 10 lines up!". Most likely thats because the whole time it was running in execution mode. It doesn't have time to investigate whats possible and think deeply about it.

Its taking a command and switching to 'procedural' mode - "Just do the thing". This produces the sub par results ~~many engineers~~ all of us have seen or are still seeing.

"Go add this new REST endpoint" - this triggers the agent to immediatly go into the procedural execution mode - with no surrounding context - whats available, 

Lets say its doing 'path finding' - its finding the fastest path to the destination! But we don't want the fastest path - we want the complete path! 

Its writing a new regex to parse the incoming email value - when that already exists. Why? Because its on the war path to complete the task in as few steps as possible.

Instead, we need to think of our process in (at least) two steps. First, we need to get the agent loaded up with all the context it needs to execute the task. THEN we can take that complete context and 'switch' the agent to execution mode and have it implement.

Instead, if you reframe how you’re working with agents to tell it to think about the problem and investigate and plan out what it’s going to do, it’ll immediately go into reasoning mode. That reasoning mode is much deeper thinking much more investigatory. Out of that you get detailed information about what needs to be done. 

What we’re trying to achieve is to get it to write a set of instructions to perform. Then, and only then, it can go execute those instructions.

## ANSWER

In this report, “AI autonomy” and “directive” are specific technical labels for how much decision‑making you hand over to Claude and how you structure your prompts. [anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/economic-index-primitives)

## AI autonomy

In the Economic Index framework, **AI autonomy** means “the degree to which users delegate decision‑making to Claude.” [anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/economic-index-primitives)
- Low autonomy: You stay in the loop, give detailed instructions, break work into steps, and review or edit Claude’s output (collaborative, “copilot” style).  
- High autonomy: You give an open‑ended or broad goal and let Claude decide many of the steps, content, and trade‑offs (agent‑like behavior). [anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/economic-index-primitives)

They track autonomy as one of five “economic primitives” alongside task complexity, skill level, use case, and success. [anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/economic-index-primitives)

## “Collaborative Mode – directive”

Since 2025, Anthropic classifies conversations into interaction types like directive, feedback loop, task iteration, validation, and learning. [anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/economic-index-march-2026-report)
These roll up into two big buckets:  
- Automation patterns (more hands‑off)  
- Augmentation patterns (more collaborative, human‑in‑the‑loop) [anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/economic-index-march-2026-report)

Within that, **directive** is a pattern where the user essentially issues a command and expects Claude to do the whole task with little back‑and‑forth. [anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/economic-index-primitives)
Examples of **directive** prompts in this sense:  
- “Write a full 2,000‑word investment memo recommending whether to acquire Company X, with sections on market, competition, and risks.”  
- “Draft a final customer email sequence for this campaign, including subject lines and CTAs.”

In these cases, the human is not iterating step by step; they’re delegating the whole artifact or decision to Claude. That’s why the earlier primitives report says rising directive use “where users delegate tasks entirely” goes along with higher AI autonomy. [anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/economic-index-primitives)

By contrast, **collaborative** or iterative patterns (like “task iteration,” “feedback loop,” “validation”) look more like:  
- “Here’s my draft memo; suggest improvements to the structure.”  
- “Propose 5 options, then we’ll refine the best one together.” [anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/economic-index-march-2026-report)

So “Collaborative Mode – directive” in your wording is a bit of a mash‑up: the report itself separates directive (automation) from iterative/feedback/validation (augmentation). In their taxonomy, directive is the **least** collaborative of those patterns. [anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/economic-index-march-2026-report)

## The sentence about “directive use patterns”

> “High tenure users are more likely to use Claude to iterate on their work, and much less likely to delegate greater responsibility through directive use patterns.” [anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/economic-index-march-2026-report)

In plain language, this means:  
- People who have been using Claude for 6+ months tend to:  
  - Bring more complex, work‑related tasks.  
  - Use Claude as a **thinking partner**: iterating, refining, and co‑editing their own drafts or ideas.  
- They are **less** likely to just say “Claude, you do the whole thing” and accept a one‑shot answer (directive, high‑autonomy usage). [anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/economic-index-march-2026-report?s=35)

Anthropic originally hypothesized that power users would do more automation, but the data show the opposite: more advanced users favor iterative, collaborative workflows over heavy delegation. [linkedin](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/startuphubai_claude-usage-diversifies-learning-curves-activity-7442269234493247490-JNg8)

If it helps, you can map your own behavior to their categories: do you mostly (a) hand Claude big, open‑ended tasks to “just do,” or (b) keep it in the loop as a coauthor/debugger you iterate with? That’s essentially the autonomy vs directive vs iterative distinction they’re measuring. [anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/economic-index-march-2026-report)
